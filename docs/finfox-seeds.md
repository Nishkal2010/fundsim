# FinFox Seed Q&A — 30 Common Student Questions

Reference answers for the FinFox AI tutor. Each answer is 2-3 sentences with at least one concrete number.

---

## Private Equity (10 questions)

## 1. How does an LBO work?

**Answer:** A leveraged buyout uses a mix of debt (typically 60–70% of the purchase price) and equity from the sponsor to acquire a company, with the target's own cash flows servicing the debt over the hold period. The sponsor controls the company through a holding structure, improves operations or grows earnings, then exits via sale or IPO after 4–7 years. The leverage amplifies equity returns because a smaller equity check earns gains on the full enterprise value appreciation.

---

## 2. What is MOIC and how is it different from IRR?

**Answer:** MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital) measures total return — if you invest $100M and get back $280M, your MOIC is 2.8x regardless of how long it took. IRR (Internal Rate of Return) is the annualized return, so a 2.8x MOIC over 5 years is roughly a 23% IRR, but the same 2.8x over 3 years is ~41% IRR. Sponsors care about both: MOIC captures magnitude, IRR captures speed, and LPs use IRR to compare across fund vintages with different hold periods.

---

## 3. How does debt paydown create value in an LBO?

**Answer:** When you buy a company with $400M of debt and $200M of equity, every dollar of debt paid down with operating cash flow shifts to equity — so retiring $200M of debt doubles your equity value before any EBITDA growth. This "deleveraging" effect means even a flat-EBITDA company can return 2x if the debt is fully paid down by exit. It's the primary reason LBO models always show a debt schedule as a central output.

---

## 4. What is a management buyout (MBO)?

**Answer:** An MBO is an LBO where the existing management team is the acquiring buyer, usually partnering with a PE sponsor who provides most of the capital. Management typically invests 1–5% of the equity check alongside the sponsor, aligning incentives to grow and improve the business. MBOs are common in corporate divestitures where management knows the asset better than any outside buyer and wants to own it outright.

---

## 5. What are add-on acquisitions and why do PE firms do them?

**Answer:** Add-on (or bolt-on) acquisitions are smaller companies bought by a PE-backed portfolio company to grow its scale, product set, or geography — a "buy-and-build" strategy. Sponsors like add-ons because they can buy fragmented smaller players at 5–7x EBITDA and exit the combined platform at 10–12x, creating "multiple expansion" purely through M&A arbitrage. A typical mid-market buyout platform might complete 3–6 add-ons over a 5-year hold.

---

## 6. How do PE sponsor economics work?

**Answer:** A PE sponsor earns two revenue streams: management fees (usually 2% per year on committed capital during the investment period, then 1.5–2% on invested capital) and carried interest (20% of profits above the hurdle rate). On a $1B fund that returns $2.2B, the carry pool might be $200–240M after returning LP capital and the preferred return. The GP typically commits 1–2% of the fund alongside LPs, aligning interests and giving them skin in the game.

---

## 7. What is carried interest (carry)?

**Answer:** Carry is the 20% performance fee that fund managers earn on profits above a negotiated hurdle (typically 8% IRR). If a fund raises $500M, returns $1.2B, and the LP hurdle is met, the $700M of profit is split roughly 80/20 — $560M to LPs and $140M to the GP as carry. Carry is the primary wealth-creation mechanism for PE professionals and is typically taxed as long-term capital gains, not ordinary income.

---

## 8. How does a PE waterfall work?

**Answer:** A waterfall is the contractual order in which fund proceeds are distributed: (1) return of LP capital, (2) preferred return to LPs at the hurdle rate (e.g., 8% IRR), (3) catch-up to the GP until they've received 20% of total profits, (4) remaining profits split 80/20. European-style waterfalls aggregate across the entire fund before paying carry; American-style waterfalls pay carry deal-by-deal, which benefits the GP but exposes LPs to clawback risk. Virtually all large-cap buyout funds use European waterfall.

---

## 9. What EBITDA multiples do PE firms typically pay?

**Answer:** Purchase price multiples vary by sector and deal size: large-cap buyouts often trade at 12–16x EBITDA, while mid-market deals (under $500M enterprise value) typically price at 7–11x. Software companies with recurring revenue can command 15–25x or higher. The entry multiple is critical because it directly determines your equity check size and the amount of value creation needed to hit return targets.

---

## 10. What are common PE exit strategies?

**Answer:** The three primary exits are (1) strategic sale to a corporate acquirer, (2) secondary buyout (sale to another PE firm), and (3) IPO. Strategic sales typically achieve the highest price because buyers pay for synergies, while secondary buyouts are faster to close. IPOs are used for larger assets and offer partial liquidity but lock up the sponsor for an additional 180-day lock-up period post-listing.

## Venture Capital (10 questions)

## 11. What is a cap table?

**Answer:** A cap table (capitalization table) is a spreadsheet listing all equity holders in a startup — founders, employees with options, and every investor — showing their ownership percentage, share class, and dollar value at various scenarios. A healthy early-stage cap table has founders owning 70-80% at seed with a 10-15% option pool carved out. The cap table is the single most important legal document for understanding who gets paid, and in what order, when the company exits.

---

## 12. What is a SAFE and how does it differ from a priced round?

**Answer:** A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is a convertible instrument that gives an investor the right to receive equity in a future priced round — it's not debt and doesn't accrue interest, but converts at a discount (typically 15–20%) or valuation cap to the next round's price. A priced round (e.g., Series A) sets an explicit share price and valuation, issuing preferred stock immediately. SAFEs are faster and cheaper to close (often under $5K in legal fees vs. $50K+ for a priced Series A) and dominate pre-seed and seed funding today.

---

## 13. How does dilution work in venture?

**Answer:** Dilution occurs every time new shares are issued — in a funding round, option pool expansion, or conversion of SAFEs/notes. If a founder owns 60% pre-Series A and the round creates 25% new shares (plus a 10% option pool refresh), the founder's stake drops to roughly 45%. Dilution isn't inherently bad — a 10% stake in a $1B company is worth more than a 100% stake in a $50M company — but founders should model dilution across multiple rounds to understand their ultimate payout.

---

## 14. What are typical valuations at seed and Series A?

**Answer:** Seed rounds in 2023-2025 typically priced companies at $8–20M pre-money valuations, with checks of $500K–$3M. Series A rounds tend to price at $20–60M pre-money with rounds of $5–15M, usually requiring $1–3M ARR or strong product-market-fit signals. These ranges vary significantly by geography (SF/NY command premiums), sector (AI commands 3–5x premiums), and market cycle.

---

## 15. What is a liquidation preference?

**Answer:** A liquidation preference gives preferred stockholders (investors) the right to get their money back before common stockholders (founders, employees) receive anything in an exit. A 1x non-participating preference means investors get their investment back first; a 2x preference means they get 2x back first. Participating preferred ("double dip") lets investors take their preference AND share in remaining proceeds pro-rata — this is heavily founder-unfavorable and largely absent from top-tier deals today.

---

## 16. What are pro-rata rights?

**Answer:** Pro-rata rights give an existing investor the right — but not the obligation — to invest in future rounds to maintain their percentage ownership. If you own 10% at seed, pro-rata rights let you invest enough in the Series A to keep your 10%. VCs prize pro-rata in winners because the best companies are oversubscribed and early investors can't normally get allocation; pro-rata is essentially an option to double down at a known price.

---

## 17. What is a term sheet?

**Answer:** A term sheet is a non-binding letter of intent summarizing the key economic and governance terms of a proposed investment — valuation, round size, share class, board composition, and protective provisions. The two most negotiated economic terms are pre-money valuation (which determines dilution) and liquidation preference. A term sheet typically has a 30-day exclusivity period to allow due diligence before the definitive documents are drafted.

---

## 18. What is the difference between pre-money and post-money valuation?

**Answer:** Pre-money valuation is the company's value before new investment; post-money is pre-money plus the new investment. If a company raises $5M at a $20M pre-money valuation, post-money is $25M, and the investor owns $5M/$25M = 20%. Most SAFE caps today are quoted as post-money caps (the YC standard since 2018), meaning the investor's percentage is calculated on the post-money value — this is more investor-friendly than pre-money SAFEs.

---

## 19. How is a VC fund structured?

**Answer:** A VC fund is a limited partnership (LP) structure: institutional LPs (pension funds, endowments, family offices) commit capital, and the GP (the VC firm) manages it for a 10-year fund life with optional 1-2 year extensions. The investment period is typically the first 3–5 years; remaining years are for follow-ons and exits. The GP commits 1–2% of the fund alongside LPs to align interests.

---

## 20. What is the "2 and 20" fee structure in VC?

**Answer:** "2 and 20" means a 2% annual management fee on committed capital and 20% carried interest on profits above the hurdle. On a $300M fund, the management fee generates $6M/year — enough to cover team salaries and operations but not wealth creation. The real money is carry: if the fund returns $900M (3x), carry on $600M of profit = $120M split among the partners. Top-tier VC firms sometimes charge 2.5/30 given oversubscribed funds and strong track records.

---

## Investment Banking (10 questions)

## 21. What is the M&A process from an IB perspective?

**Answer:** In a sell-side M&A process, the bank prepares marketing materials, runs a structured auction (broad process: 50–100 initial contacts; focused process: 5–10 strategic parties), manages NDAs and management presentations, and negotiates binding bids toward a signing and close. A typical sell-side process takes 4–6 months from mandate to signing, with another 2–4 months to regulatory close. The bank earns a success fee of 1–3% of transaction value (higher for sub-$500M deals), paid only at close.

---

## 22. How does a DCF work?

**Answer:** A DCF (Discounted Cash Flow) values a company by projecting its free cash flows for 5–10 years, then adding a terminal value (usually a Gordon Growth Model or exit multiple), and discounting everything back to present value at the WACC (weighted average cost of capital). If a company generates $50M of FCF growing at 10%/year, with a 10x terminal EBITDA multiple and a 12% WACC, the DCF might imply a $600–700M enterprise value. DCF is highly sensitive to terminal value assumptions, which often represent 60–80% of total value.

---

## 23. What is comparable company analysis (comps)?

**Answer:** Comps value a company by applying trading multiples from publicly listed peers — typically EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, and P/E — to the subject company's financials. If five comparable software companies trade at 8–12x NTM revenue and your company has $100M revenue, the comps range implies $800M–$1.2B enterprise value. The art is in selecting truly comparable peers and applying discounts/premiums for size, growth, and margins.

---

## 24. What is accretion/dilution analysis?

**Answer:** Accretion/dilution analysis tests whether an acquisition increases ("accretes") or decreases ("dilutes") the acquirer's earnings per share (EPS). If the deal is EPS accretive, the acquired earnings (adjusted for synergies and deal costs) exceed the cost of capital used to fund the acquisition; if dilutive, EPS falls. A deal is typically accretive if the target's P/E multiple is lower than the acquirer's and synergies are meaningful — a common rule of thumb is that $1 of cost synergy creates ~$1.50–$2 of deal value.

---

## 25. What is a fairness opinion?

**Answer:** A fairness opinion is a letter from an investment bank stating that the consideration in a proposed transaction is fair, from a financial point of view, to the shareholders of the target (or acquirer). Boards of public companies are legally required to obtain one before recommending a transaction to shareholders to protect against fiduciary duty claims. The opinion is based on valuation analyses including DCF, comps, and precedent transactions — it does not opine on whether the deal is strategically the best choice, only whether the price is financially fair.

---

## 26. How does an LBO analysis differ from a standard DCF in IB?

**Answer:** In an LBO analysis (done by IB bankers in buy-side contexts), the model focuses on equity returns to the financial sponsor — IRR and MOIC — rather than enterprise value to all capital providers. The LBO model explicitly tracks debt schedules, mandatory amortization, cash sweeps, and the sponsor's equity at exit, solving backward from a target IRR (typically 20–25%) to determine the maximum price a PE firm can pay. Bankers use LBO analysis to set a "financial buyer floor" valuation alongside the strategic buyer range from comps and DCF.

---

## 27. What goes in a pitch book?

**Answer:** A pitch book (or "book") is the presentation a bank prepares to win an M&A or capital markets mandate — it typically includes an industry overview, strategic rationale for the transaction, preliminary valuation (comps, DCF, precedents), a process recommendation, and the bank's credentials and team. Pitch books for sell-side M&A mandates are called "bake-off" or "beauty contest" books and are delivered to 3–5 competing banks. Junior bankers spend enormous time building and formatting these; a typical pitch book is 40–80 slides.

---

## 28. What is a roadshow?

**Answer:** A roadshow is the 10–14 day marketing sprint before an IPO or bond offering where company management and bankers meet with institutional investors in major financial centers (New York, Boston, San Francisco, London) to pitch the investment. The goal is to build the order book — gauge demand and set a final offering price — which is typically set the night before trading begins. IPO roadshows are tightly scripted to comply with SEC quiet period rules; management cannot say anything not in the prospectus (S-1).

---

## 29. What is a tombstone in banking?

**Answer:** A tombstone (formally called a "tombstone advertisement" or "deal announcement") is the publicly released notice announcing a completed transaction — showing the parties, deal size, and the advisors' logos ("box" or "credit") in a formal layout. Banks collect tombstones to demonstrate deal experience and use them to populate league tables, which rank banks by total deal volume or number of transactions in a given category. Tombstones historically were printed as physical plaques given to deal team members as mementos.

---

## 30. What are league tables and why do they matter?

**Answer:** League tables rank investment banks by M&A advisory, equity underwriting, or debt underwriting volume — published quarterly by data providers like Refinitiv and Bloomberg. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley consistently top global M&A league tables, typically each advising on $500B–$1T+ of transactions annually. Banks obsess over league table rankings because Fortune 500 CFOs and boards use them to shortlist advisors — being ranked #1 in a sector is worth hundreds of millions in incremental mandates.
