# LBO Mechanics in Plain English: How Private Equity Actually Makes Money

Private equity gets a lot of mystique. "They buy companies, load them with debt, and flip them for a profit." That's technically true but leaves out everything interesting. Here's how an LBO actually works — and why leverage is a double-edged sword.

---

## What Is an LBO?

A leveraged buyout is an acquisition financed mostly with debt. The buyer — typically a PE firm — puts up a fraction of the purchase price in equity and borrows the rest. The acquired company's own cash flows are used to pay down that debt over time.

Think of it like buying a rental property with a mortgage. You put down 20%, borrow 80%, and the rent covers the loan payments. If the property appreciates, you sell and your return on that 20% down payment is enormous — because you controlled the full asset with a fraction of the capital.

---

## The Debt/Equity Split

In a typical LBO, 60–70% of the purchase price is debt. The PE firm puts in the remaining 30–40% as equity. That debt sits on the company's balance sheet, not the PE firm's — which is why the target company's cash flow quality matters so much. Stable, predictable cash flows can support heavy debt service. Cyclical businesses can't.

---

## How Returns Compound

PE firms make money three ways in an LBO:

1. **Debt paydown.** As the company generates cash and repays debt, equity value grows. If you buy a $100M company with $65M in debt, and the company pays down $30M of that debt over five years, your equity stake is now worth $35M more — without the company growing at all.

2. **Multiple expansion.** If you buy at 6x EBITDA and sell at 8x EBITDA, the difference drops almost entirely to equity. PE firms work to grow the business and improve its story to justify a higher exit multiple.

3. **EBITDA growth.** Operational improvements — cutting costs, expanding margins, making add-on acquisitions — grow the earnings base itself. A higher EBITDA at the same multiple means a higher exit price.

---

## A Simple Example

You buy a company for $100M (6x $16.7M EBITDA). You put in $35M equity and borrow $65M.

Over five years, the company grows EBITDA to $25M and pays down $20M of debt. You sell at 7x EBITDA:

- Exit value: $175M
- Remaining debt: $45M
- Equity proceeds: $130M
- On your $35M investment, that's ~3.7x MOIC and roughly 30% IRR

If instead EBITDA had stayed flat and the multiple compressed to 5x, exit equity would be around $45M — a 1.3x MOIC. Same entry, completely different outcome.

---

## Why Leverage Amplifies Risk

Leverage magnifies returns in both directions. If the business hits a downturn and can't service its debt, the company can go into distress or default — and equity holders get wiped out first. The banks have seniority. The PE firm loses everything.

That's why deal selection matters more than financial engineering. The best LBOs aren't the most levered — they're the most defensible businesses, bought at fair prices, with credible paths to value creation.
